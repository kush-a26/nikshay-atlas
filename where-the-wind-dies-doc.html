How it began

A third-year lecture I did not expect to remember.

In my third year of MBBS, a professor of Community Medicine told us something that stayed. He said TB is curable — has been for decades. He said the drugs work.

And then he said: the problem isn't the medicine, it's the disappearing patient.

He called them "defaulters." I later learned the preferred term is Lost to Follow-Up — LTFU. The patient starts treatment, shows up for a few weeks, and then simply stops.

What I was told

"It's not laziness. It's logistics."

When I asked why patients stopped, the answers were never about willpower. They were about geography. About money. About seasonal labour migration. About a government clinic 40 kilometres away, requiring three bus changes.

The DOTS protocol — Directly Observed Treatment, Short-Course — requires patients to show up and be watched taking their medication. Every single day. For six months.

For a daily wage labourer in rural Rajasthan, that is not a health decision. That is an economic calculation they cannot afford to lose.

My previous project

I had already been thinking about this.

Before this project, I made TB is in the Air — a tangible data visualisation using samara seeds to encode global TB burden, drug resistance, testing coverage, and treatment outcomes across G20 nations.

The orange came from Rifampin — the essential TB drug that tints tears and urine a warm orange-red. A visible sign of treatment at work.

This project is the same question, asked at a different resolution. Not the globe. The district. The block. The person who decides where the field team goes this week.

The design question

Is dropout random, or does it have a pattern?

If it has a pattern — and I suspected it did — then it can be mapped. And if it can be mapped, it can be acted on.

That became the project.

02 Understanding the Problem
What dropout means

When a patient disappears from the system, they do not disappear from the disease.

Tuberculosis treatment is a six-month minimum regimen. In India's public health system, this means enrolling in NTEP — the National Tuberculosis Elimination Programme — and attending a Designated Microscopy Centre (DMC) or a DOTS provider, where a health worker watches you take your medication.

When a patient stops treatment before completing the regimen, they are classified as Lost to Follow-Up (LTFU). This is not merely an administrative problem. It is a clinical catastrophe in slow motion.

The bacteria that remain after an incomplete course are not cured. They are survivors. And they carry with them partial resistance to the drugs that did not finish the job.

What is being done

NTEP, Nikshay, and the limits of tabular data.

India's NTEP — formerly RNTCP — is one of the most ambitious public health programmes in the world. Its digital backbone, Nikshay, tracks every enrolled patient from notification to outcome. In 2023 alone, 2.6 million cases were notified through the system.

Nikshay also manages Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT): the ₹500/month Nikshay Poshan Yojana nutritional support every TB patient is entitled to. When this payment fails — due to incorrect bank details, system errors, or administrative delays — the incentive to continue treatment disappears with it.

But here is what Nikshay does not do: it does not tell a District TB Officer which block is failing and why. It produces tabular reports. It does not surface the spatial pattern of dropout drivers — the clustering of distant clinics, high migration corridors, payment gaps — that would let a programme manager make a targeted, evidence-based decision about where to deploy limited resources.

That is the gap this project addresses.

The Decision Maker

The DTO holds the district. But works with a spreadsheet.

The District TB Officer is responsible for planning, training, supervising and monitoring the NTEP across all blocks in the district. They allocate Senior Treatment Supervisors, coordinate ASHA field visits, manage DBT payment processing, and ensure drug supply to DMCs.

Every Monday, the DTO receives the Nikshay weekly report: a table of patient counts, treatment outcomes, notification numbers. Block-level dropout rates exist in that data. But the cause of dropout requires manual cross-referencing across multiple formats.

No DTO has time for that. The nuance disappears, and resource allocation becomes broad rather than surgical.

Why the DTO?

The district is the smallest unit of real intervention.

State-level officers set policy. Block-level supervisors execute it. But the DTO sits at the hinge — the only person who has both the authority to re-deploy field resources and the data to justify the decision.

A tool designed for the DTO needs to surface pattern, not data. It needs to collapse the cognitive load of comparing 12 blocks across 5 variables into a single spatial view with a diagnostic output.

That is the design specification. Everything else follows from this.

04 — In the Field
RM
Dr. Ramesh Meena District TB Officer · Udaipur District, Rajasthan · Interviewed during internship, 2023
Reconstructed composite account based on fieldwork during MBBS internship at a district health facility in 2023. Name is fictional; observations are drawn from direct conversation with NTEP programme officers.
On what his Monday report looks like
"I get a table. Columns for each block — notification count, treatment success, LTFU, deaths. I know which blocks are red. I've known for months. But I have twelve Senior Treatment Supervisors and I can't send all of them to Jhadol block just because the number is high. I need to know why it's high. The table doesn't tell me why."
On migration and seasonal dropout
"The harvest season is the worst. October to March. We lose patients — not to death, to movement. They go to Gujarat, to construction sites in Surat. When they leave, Nikshay loses them. Their address becomes invalid. The ASHA worker has no number to call. They get classified as LTFU, but they're not lost — they're just somewhere else."
On the DBT payment problem
"The ₹500 per month is important. For a malnourished patient in a tribal block, that money is the difference between eating and not eating. When the payment fails — and it fails often — the patient stops coming within a month. They have no reason to make the journey."
On what a tool like this atlas would mean
"If I could see the map — this block has high LTFU because of distance, this one because of migration — I could give targeted instructions. Not 'visit all blocks.' 'Go to Kherwara, check DBT payment records.' 'In Jhadol, start the inter-district transfer forms before October.' That is actionable."

05 The Five Risk Signals
Research-grounded variables

Each signal is documented in peer-reviewed literature as a predictor of LTFU.

The design decision was not which signals exist — the literature is clear — but how to render them spatially so they are immediately actionable for a non-data-scientist programme officer.

Signal 01

Distance to DMC

10 km Threshold beyond which LTFU rises sharply

The Designated Microscopy Centre is where patients collect medication under DOTS. Distance to DMC is the single most consistently documented predictor of dropout in rural India.

Beyond 10 km, the probability of LTFU increases in a dose-response pattern. In tribal blocks of Rajasthan (Kotra, Kherwara, Jhadol), distances can exceed 50 km with no public transport during monsoon.

BMC Infectious Diseases · 2010
Risk Factors for Treatment Default among New Smear Positive TB Patients under DOTS in India
Vijay et al. — PMC2850369
"Majority of risk factors for default were treatment and provider oriented and rectifiable with appropriate interventions — including distance to DOTS facility as a primary structural barrier."
Distance to DOTS facility vs. LTFU rate — illustrative trend from literature
Trend derived from Vijay et al. 2010, Patidar et al. 2021, and NTEP district reports. Values illustrative.
Signal 02

Seasonal Migration

Oct–Mar Peak dropout season — aligned with construction & harvest migration

Seasonal migrants — daily wage workers who follow construction, harvest, and brick-kiln cycles — are the single highest-risk group for LTFU nationally.

When they move, their Nikshay address becomes invalid. The ASHA worker has no forwarding information. The patient falls through the gap between two bureaucracies.

PLOS ONE · 2021
Reasons for LTFU of Pulmonary TB Patients: A Qualitative Study, Madhya Pradesh
Patidar et al. — PMC8699669
"Fear of losing a job and migration within the country for work are key factors responsible for losing follow-up. Most LTFU patients are primary earners or in economically active ages."
Times of India · 2023
"Migrant workers account for disproportionate share of TB treatment defaults in high-burden states"
Programme officers in Bihar, Rajasthan and UP report predictable annual dropout spikes in October–March, coinciding with peak inter-state labour movement.
Signal 03

DBT Payment Gap

4–8 wks Time window within which dropout follows a missed NPY payment

The Nikshay Poshan Yojana provides ₹500/month to every enrolled TB patient — nutritional support intended to compensate for the caloric cost of treatment.

When payments fail — due to bank linkage errors, system lags, or administrative backlogs — patients drop out within 4–8 weeks. In Niwari district, DBT coverage dropped from 87.5% to 49.4% in a single year, immediately correlated with increased LTFU.

Tuberculosis Research and Treatment · 2024
Estimate of TB Incidence and Critical Analysis of Programmatic Data — Sub-National Certification, District Niwari, M.P.
PMC12349803
"Nikshay Poshan Yojana beneficiary payments declined from 87.5% in 2021 to 49.4% in 2022, a drop correlated with treatment discontinuation in affected blocks."
Signal 04

Retreatment Proportion

Cat II Prior defaulters — peak LTFU at days 45–90 of intensive phase

A high retreatment proportion signals accumulated failure — patients who have defaulted before and returned to treatment. These patients are at significantly higher risk of defaulting again.

Clinical evidence shows LTFU in retreatment cases peaks specifically between days 45–90 — the window when side effects are most pronounced and the initial enthusiasm of restarting has faded.

PMC · 2013
Impact of Isoniazid Resistance on Treatment Outcomes of Smear Positive Retreatment TB, Andhra Pradesh
PMC3795751
"Among isoniazid-resistant retreatment cases, 44% had poor treatment outcomes — 17% default, 15% death. Retreatment patients demonstrate significantly higher LTFU than new cases."
Signal 05

Private Sector Share

60% Estimated share of TB patients who first present to private sector

Most TB care in India begins in the private sector. When patients transition to the public NTEP system (required for free medication), they face a point of maximum fragility.

A block with very low private sector share is paradoxically at high risk — patients have no alternative pathway if the public facility is inaccessible.

BMC Infectious Diseases · 2017
Tuberculosis Treatment Outcomes among Disadvantaged Patients in India
PMC5493095
"In India, more than 70% of health care contacts occur in the private sector. Socio-economic position is associated with increased risk of TB and loss to follow-up."
India TB Report 2024
The private sector contributed nearly 33% of the 25.5 lakh reported TB cases in 2023 — up from 1.9 lakh in 2015. Private sector engagement remains highest in urban blocks where formal notification pathways exist.
Design Decisions

Why a map? Why block-level? Why the DTO and not the patient?

Why a map: Tabular data requires the reader to hold spatial relationships in memory. A map performs that synthesis automatically — spatial clustering of dropout risk becomes visually obvious in a way that a ranked list cannot achieve.

Why block-level: Blocks are the smallest unit at which an NTEP programme officer has direct authority to re-deploy resources. The block is the intervention unit.

Why the DTO and not the patient: The patient needs counselling, financial support, ASHA contact. The DTO needs to know which blocks need which types of intervention this week. Clarity of user scope was the first and most important design decision.

Why "Where the Wind Dies": TB is airborne. "TB is in the Air" — my previous project — established this register. Where the treatment system fails — where patients drop out, where the programme loses momentum — that is where the wind dies into silence. Not metaphorically. Epidemiologically.

Honest Limitations

What this tool does not do — and what a production version would need.

Data is illustrative. The block-level TB data in the atlas is plausible and research-grounded but not drawn from actual Nikshay records. A production deployment would require API access to Nikshay's district-level outcome database.

The map boundaries are approximate. Block polygons are positioned using real lat/lng coordinates for block headquarters, rendered as circles. Actual administrative boundary polygons require Census 2011 sub-district shapefiles.

What v2 would look like: Real-time Nikshay API pull → actual block GeoJSON → inter-district migration corridor visualisation → DBT payment failure alert layer → mobile-first for field STS use.
